Winners, or fewer errors?

What actually separates the winner from the loser in a Grand Slam match — and does it depend on where they're playing.

An investigation using 712 men's singles matches from the 2026 Australian Open, Roland-Garros and Wimbledon · by tynelee

The question

Tennis rewards two opposite instincts. You can go for the lines and hit outright winners, or you can play it safer and simply wait for your opponent to make the mistake. Which philosophy actually wins matches: aggression, or consistency?

Winners and unforced errors are two sides of the same coin. A player going for more winners will naturally commit more errors chasing them; a player playing it safe will hit fewer winners in exchange for a tidier scoreline. Comparing how much each margin swings toward the match winner is a reasonable proxy for asking whether taking on risk pays off at the top of the men's game.

To find out, I collected match statistics for every completed men's singles match at the three 2026 Grand Slams played so far — the Australian Open, Roland-Garros, and Wimbledon — using the API-Tennis get_fixtures endpoint. Restricting to Grand Slams keeps player quality and match formats consistent, while still covering all three major playing surfaces.

Finding one

Across all matches, it's closer than you'd think

For every match, I calculated how many more winners the eventual winner hit than their opponent, and separately, how many fewer unforced errors they made. If aggression were clearly the winning formula, the winners margin would dwarf the errors margin. It doesn't.

Finding two

The surface changes the story

Surface is the variable most unique to tennis. Grass, at Wimbledon, is fast and low-bouncing, rewarding big serves and aggressive shot-making. Clay, at Roland-Garros, is slow and high-bouncing, extending rallies and rewarding patience. The hard courts of the Australian Open sit in between. Splitting the same comparison by tournament shows the pattern isn't fixed — it bends toward whichever style the surface actually rewards.

This lines up with what clay is supposed to do to a match. Slower bounces mean rallies run longer, which rewards the player still standing at the end of a long exchange more than the player who ended it early with a clean winner.

Finding three

Take away the serve, and the two styles look almost identical

There's a catch in the numbers above. Raw "winners" totals include aces, and raw "unforced errors" totals include double faults — both of which measure serving quality, not rally risk-taking. An ace is a winner you hit without playing a single rally shot. To isolate the actual aggression-versus-consistency trade-off, I recalculated both margins with aces and double faults stripped out, leaving only what happened once the ball was in play.

Once the serve is stripped out, most of the "aggression advantage" turns out to be a serving advantage.

This is the most useful finding of the three. It suggests that a meaningful part of what looks like an edge for aggressive players is really an edge for good servers — two things that correlate but aren't the same skill. Once you remove serving from the picture, hitting more winners in the rally and making fewer unforced errors in the rally come out close to tied.

So, which wins matches?

Both styles can win. Serving well matters more than either.

Across all 712 matches, the winner typically holds a slightly larger margin in extra winners hit (+7) than in errors avoided (+6), but the overlap between the two distributions is too wide to call this decisive on its own. Split by surface, the pattern behaves the way anyone who follows tennis would expect: clay rewards fewer errors over the winners tally, while the faster hard and grass courts tip slightly the other way.

The most telling result comes from separating serve statistics out of the picture entirely. Once aces and double faults are removed, the winners margin collapses toward the errors margin, and the two nearly match. That points to serving, not raw risk-taking in the rally, as the larger factor behind the "aggression wins" narrative.

In short: both playing philosophies can win a Grand Slam match. The clearer differentiator, at least in this dataset, is how well a player serves — not whether they choose to attack or play it safe once the point is underway.
Limitations of this analysis

This is associative, not causal. Tennis outcomes are shaped by matchups, tactics, fitness, and conditions that this dataset can't capture, so a margin difference between winners and losers doesn't prove that one style causes wins.

The dataset covers men's singles at three 2026 Grand Slams only. Results may not generalise to the WTA tour, to lower-tier events, or to earlier seasons with different player fields.

16 matches with pre-match withdrawals were excluded for having no recorded statistics, which slightly reduces the sample from the full draw size but shouldn't bias the comparison, since these matches never produced any rally play to measure.
